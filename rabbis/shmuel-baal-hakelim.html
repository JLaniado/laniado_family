ר׳ שמואל לניאדו ״בעל הכלים״
Rabbi Shmuel Laniado, "Ba'al HaKelim" (Master of the Vessels)
Died 1605/1611 · Generation 3 · Chief Rabbi of Aleppo, ~40 years

Rabbi Shmuel, son of Rabbi Avraham "HaDarshan," is the best-documented of the early Laniado rabbis — and the reason the family's name became attached to Aleppo for the next two centuries.

The barrel of diamonds

Family tradition tells that Rabbi Shmuel was once traveling by ship — possibly from Tzfat to Aleppo — when a fellow passenger died during the voyage. The man's belongings, including several barrels, were sold off. Rabbi Shmuel bought a barrel supposedly containing fish; when he opened it, he found it full of diamonds and other precious stones, and became a wealthy man. Some say this is why nearly every book he later wrote carries the word כלי (kli, "vessel") in its title — in gratitude to God for the miracle. The details vary between tellings, but the family holds the core of the story to be true. According to Rabbi Yosef She'io, Rabbi Shmuel moved to Israel and settled in Tzfat around the Hebrew year רצ״ז (roughly 1537).

Sent by the Beit Yosef

In Tzfat, Rabbi Shmuel studied under an extraordinary roster of teachers: Rabbi Yaakov Beirav, the RaMaK, the Ari, Rabbi Chaim Vital, Rabbi Shlomo Alkabetz (author of Lecha Dodi), Rabbi Yisrael di Curiel and his son-in-law Rabbi Yisrael Najara, Rabbi Moshe Alshich, Rabbi Shlomo Sagis, Rabbi Elazar Bar Yochai, and Rabbi Avraham Galanti, among others he quotes hearing from directly.

When the community of Aleppo wrote to the Beit Yosef asking him to become their Chief Rabbi, he replied that he was too occupied — some say he was then in the midst of writing the שולחן ערוך — but promised to send "ish asher kamoni," איש אשר כמוני, "a man who is like me." That man was Rabbi Shmuel Laniado. Although Aleppo's native families traditionally drew their Chief Rabbi only from בית דוד (the House of David), the Beit Yosef's endorsement was enough: Rabbi Shmuel was accepted fully, and served as Chief Rabbi of Aleppo for almost 40 years.

We know he lived a long life because the MaHarit cites him in a responsum concerning a case of agunah. His grandson Rabbi Refael Shlomo later wrote:

"I learnt from my grandfather, who was always fully involved in Torah — the give-and-take of Abaye and Rava — while remaining calm and peaceful, focused on helping the community grow in Torah, writing sefarim, and publicizing the sefarim of earlier sages. That is how he lived."

There are also three responsa in אבקת רוכל in which Rabbi Shmuel disputes a point of halacha with another scholar, and the Beit Yosef sides with him.

His writings

  • כלי חמדה (Kli Chemdah) — on the Torah
  • כלי יקר (Kli Yakar) — on the early Prophets
  • כלי פז (Kli Paz) — on the Book of Isaiah
  • כלי גולה (Kli Golah) — not extant; likely on Eichah (Lamentations)
  • שכל טוב (Sechel Tov) — on Midrash Socher Tov
  • מבקש ה׳ (Mevakesh Hashem) — on the Torah; not extant

The Chida praised these works highly, noting that they differ from other Tanach commentaries in weaving a deep Kabbalistic dimension into their reading of Midrash and the biblical text.

In 1863, Rabbi Yoel Bloch compiled all of Rabbi Shmuel's discourses on Tehillim from Kli Chemdah into a new work, תרועת המלך. In 1931, Rabbi David Ben Tzion Laniado — eight generations removed from the "Ba'al HaKelim" — prepared it for a new, expanded printing; the manuscript was stolen on the way home from the printer, though some pages were later recovered and published by his son, Rabbi Shlomo Laniado, in 1978.
