ר׳ רפאל שלמה לניאדו ״בית דינו של שלמה״ ״מהרש״ל״
Rabbi Refael Shlomo Laniado, "Ba'al Beit Dino Shel Shlomo" / "MaHaRaShal"
17th century · Generation 7 · Dayan and leader of Aleppo, 54 years

Rabbi Refael Shlomo was the eldest son of Rabbi Shmuel "HaMufla." In his youth he studied under Rabbi Shmuel Dweck HaCohen, one of the leading sages and Dayanim of Aleppo at the time. He went on to serve as a Dayan and leader of Aleppo for 54 years, sitting on the Beit Din alongside Rabbi Yehuda Katzin (MaHari Katzin), and succeeding his father after his passing.

He is known by the acronym MaHaRaShal and by the titles ״הרב המובהק״ and ״סיניור חכם״. His father, Rabbi Shmuel, gave him a formal endorsement (haskama) stating that "his life is entirely Torah, without any other motive, and you should rely on him for practical halacha." His own son, Rabbi Efraim, later wrote: "for every page my father wrote in Torah, he could have written a thousand more."

"Rabbi Shlomo ruled with an iron fist, and everyone respected and listened to everything he said. He was not afraid to stand against anyone — even the richest and most powerful people in the community — if they went against halacha he opposed them, even when the majority of the community was doing wrong."

Rabbi Eliyahu Dweck HaCohen said of him that everyone — from the simplest person to the greatest Torah scholars, across Babylon, Baghdad, and the surrounding lands — knew him and brought their most difficult questions to him. One account tells of three violent men in the community, said to have killed other Jews, who threatened to torture and kill Rabbi Shlomo if he did not rule in their favor in a court case. He answered: "I will do whatever it takes to uphold the Torah and halacha, no matter what the case is." He was not harmed, and — the sources say — this was not the only time he faced such pressure and held firm. Despite his authority, he is remembered as remarkably humble, and was careful never to issue a ruling without writing it down in full, so it could not later be misrepresented.

Anecdotes

The funeral of a simple man. When an ordinary member of the community passed away, custom held that the rabbi would walk a few feet with the body and then leave — but Rabbi Shlomo walked the entire way. Afterward, his son Rabbi Efraim asked why, since his father had never done so before, especially not for someone unremarkable. Rabbi Shlomo answered that, while walking, he had seen large groups of angels accompanying the deceased and singing Tehillim, with one radiant figure who resembled King David leading them — "how could I leave?" Rabbi Efraim, still puzzled why this simple man merited such an honor, prayed for an answer and that night dreamed of a stranger who explained: the man had known only the first book of Tehillim, and every day after work he would go to the synagogue and repeat it. For that quiet devotion, he merited what he received at his funeral.

The plague. During a severe plague in Aleppo, Rabbi Shlomo wrote certain Divine Names on parchment and gave them to the synagogue's shamash (caretaker), instructing him to go to the synagogue at night and, when he heard the spirits approaching, to hold up the parchment. Frightened, the shamash hesitated, but Rabbi Shlomo promised no harm would come to him and that he would earn great merit. When he did as instructed, he heard the spirits say, "oh no — now we are out of a job," and the plague stopped. Rabbi Shlomo then had the parchment shown to those already ill, and they recovered.

The shofar. Rabbi Shlomo served as chazzan and shofar-blower of the Great Synagogue of Aleppo. Toward the end of his life, too weak to attend, he appointed his son Rabbi Efraim to take his place. Afterward, congregants told Rabbi Shlomo the prayers had gone well, but the start of the shofar blowing had felt weak. When Rabbi Efraim came home, his father asked what happened; Efraim explained that as he recited the prayer asking for an angel to be appointed over the shofar blowing, the synagogue suddenly filled with angels, and he grew frightened. His father replied: "You invited the angels to come, and you didn't expect them to? Of course they came — what's the surprise?"

The Chida praises him in many places, calling him סבא דמשפט and other titles of honor, and even suggests some of his rulings were divinely inspired. He frequently debated points of Torah with Rabbi Shimon Dweck HaCohen — author of ריח שדה — particularly regarding the timing of divorce documents (get) and antedated legal documents; when several of these questions were sent to Rabbi Yona Navon for arbitration, he ruled in Rabbi Shlomo's favor each time.
